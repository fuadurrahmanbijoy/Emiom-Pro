# Intellectual Guide: Writing an Introductory Business Studies Book

*Reference for content, tone, depth, and structure. Field: Business, Management, Accounting, Micro-economics, Finance. Audience: first-year university students. Every decision below comes from your answers.*

---

## 1. The Approach in One Paragraph

Each idea is taught in **layers**: the idea, then an example, then the rule, then the exceptions. Topics usually open with a **puzzle or question**. Explanations are never hurried, and length depends on importance, with a floor of three visible lines. Examples come from two tracks: a **documented running business** and **real historical cases**. Every calculation is worked by hand, step by step, because students will sit exams. Referencing is light by default and moderate for defining topics. Every chapter closes with the same set of features.

---

## 2. Voice: One Book, Four Registers

The book has one consistent character: serious, clear, and never condescending. The register shifts with the job being done.

| Register | Use it when | How it sounds |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Storyteller** | Introducing a new, complex idea | Opens with a puzzle or a moment of tension; builds the idea so the reader wants the answer |
| **Plain authority** | Stating definitions, rules, and principles | Short, exact sentences; no ornament |
| **Patient tutor** | Worked examples and procedures | "We" and "you"; each step announced and explained |
| **Measured professor** | Framing, history, and judgement | Dignified, slightly formal, suited to the Victorian look of the book |

**Language rules**

- Average sentence about 18-22 words. Vary it; a short sentence after a long one lands well.
- Define a term before using it. Introduce a term once, in bold italic, then use it the same way throughout.
- Avoid idioms and slang that may not travel across cultures or languages.
- Write in the active voice unless the actor truly does not matter.
- Never hurry. If a step matters, give it room.
- No jokes at the reader's expense; wit is welcome only when it helps an idea stick.

---

## 3. Which Approach for Which Topic

| Kind of topic | Open with | Then |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A new, complex idea** (something the learner has never met) | A puzzle or question, told in storyteller mode | Build the idea in layers; end with the plain rule |
| **An existing framework or phenomenon** (market structures, financial statements) | A puzzle, then a concrete example | Draw out the general principle; state the rule; note exceptions |
| **A technique or procedure** (journal entries, break-even, NPV) | A concrete example | Worked example in tutor mode; formula afterward |
| **A defining theory with a known origin** (division of labour, double-entry) | A short historical hook | Explain the idea; connect it to a modern case |

When in doubt, start with a question the reader can almost answer.

---

## 4. Anatomy of a Concept (the Layered Spine)

Most concepts follow this order:

1. **Idea.** What it is, in plain words, and why it matters. Opens with a puzzle where suitable.
2. **Example.** A concrete case, from the running business or from history.
3. **Rule.** The definition, principle, or formula, stated precisely.
4. **Exceptions.** Where it fails, limits, and common confusions.

Weave the other three approaches in briefly, so the understanding is thorough:

- **By questions** (What is it? Why does it exist? How does it work? Where does it fail?). Use these to check that no layer is missing.
- **By viewpoint** (owner, manager, employee, customer). A sentence or short paragraph showing how the idea looks from another seat.
- **By stages** (simple, then detailed, then advanced). Give the simple version first; add detail only after the simple one is secure. Mark advanced material clearly so a beginner can defer it.

---

## 5. How Long Each Piece Should Be

Length follows importance. It should never feel hurried, and it should never feel padded.

| Tier | What it covers | Typical length |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Minor term** | Vocabulary and small distinctions | 3 to 8 lines (**never fewer than three visible lines**), plus a side note |
| **Standard concept** | Most ideas in a chapter | 150-300 words |
| **Core idea** | The idea the section exists to teach | 400-700 words, with an example and exceptions |
| **Landmark topic** | A defining idea of the discipline | 800-1,500 words, with history, worked example, subsections, and moderate citation |

**Other proportions**

- **Paragraphs:** one central idea each, 4-10 lines. Write so that every paragraph can be summarised in a single line, because the side column will carry that summary.
- **Sections:** 3-6 per chapter.
- **Chapters:** about 8,000-12,000 words, or roughly 15-20 A4 pages at this density.
- **Opening puzzle:** about half to one page.

---

## 6. Examples: Two Tracks

**Track A: The running business.** The student "builds" one business across the book. Present it as a **documented case file**, not a story: dates, figures, decisions, and ledger entries, with no dialogue, invented characters' feelings, or narrative colour. It should grow in an order that matches the subjects:

- Business basics: choosing an idea and structure.
- Management: organising people and work.
- Accounting: recording transactions and preparing statements.
- Micro-economics: pricing, costs, demand, competition.
- Finance: funding, investment decisions, risk.

Label it clearly as **illustrative**, so no reader mistakes it for a real company.

**Track B: Real historical cases.** Use real, dated, verified events to show an idea at work (for example, the origins of double-entry bookkeeping, or early ideas of scientific management). Keep them short, factual, and attributed.

**Rules for both**

- Never invent history. If a date, figure, or quote cannot be verified, leave it out.
- State which country's rules apply when laws, tax, or standards differ by place (for example, accounting standards).
- Real company figures go out of date; prefer historical cases with settled facts, and date any current figures.

## 7. Numbers and Calculations

Students must be able to do this by hand, in exams. Never underestimate them and never skip a step.

**Every formula or calculation gets a worked example** laid out in the same order every time:

1. **Given.** List the data.
2. **Find.** State what is asked.
3. **Formula.** Write it in words, then in symbols.
4. **Steps.** Show every line of arithmetic, including units.
5. **Answer.** State it in a sentence.
6. **Check.** Show a quick test that the answer is sensible.

**Progression**

- First example: small, clean numbers, so the method is the only thing to learn.
- Second example: slightly messier, realistic numbers.
- Then a note on **common slips** (wrong sign, forgotten units, mixed-up periods).
- Practice problems close the chapter.

Explain where a formula comes from in a line or two when it helps, but full derivations are for landmark formulas only.

---

## 8. Referencing

- **Default: light.** Name the thinker and year in the text (for example, "Fayol, 1916") and list a few sources as further reading.
- **Defining topics: moderate.** Cite the founder or landmark work by name, with a short note on why it mattered, in a dedicated **Sources** section at the end of the chapter.
- **Clarifications and side points** go in footnotes.
- **One consolidated reading list** goes at the back of the book.
- Do not quote long passages from other works; paraphrase and credit.

---

## 9. Every Chapter Has the Same Shape

1. **Chapter opener** (full-page design from the visual guide).
2. **The opening puzzle:** a question or situation that makes the chapter necessary.
3. **Sections** built from concepts, each following the layered spine.
4. **A worked case** from the running business.
5. **The closing pack**, always in this order:
   - **Summary:** a few lines.
   - **Think it through:** a short problem or case.
   - **Where people go wrong:** common misunderstandings, stated and corrected.
   - **In history:** a true account of how this idea solved or saved something significant.
   - **Sources:** only for defining topics.
6. **Review questions** with short answers collected at the back of the book.

**Cross-references:** business subjects interlock (a cost in accounting is a cost in economics). When an idea appears in another chapter, point to it in the side column or a footnote.

---

## 10. Writing Checklist for Each Section

- Does it open with a question or an example before the rule?
- Is every term defined before it is used, and marked in bold italic once?
- Is each paragraph about one idea, summarisable in a line?
- Does every concept reach its exceptions, not only its rule?
- Is each explanation at least three full lines, and long enough to feel unhurried?
- Is every calculation worked in full, in the standard order?
- Is every historical claim verified?
- Would a student with no prior knowledge follow each step without guessing?

---

## 11. Open Choices Still to Confirm

- The type of business for the running case (a bakery, a workshop, a trading firm, or another).
- Which country's standards and currency the book uses by default, and whether to add notes for other systems.
- Whether the review answers sit at the back of the book or at the end of each chapter.
