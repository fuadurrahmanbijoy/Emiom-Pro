The Idea of Partnership

Two people, each lacking something, decide to join forces.

Imagine that two friends, one skilled at baking and the other at selling, decide to open a small bakery together. Neither can afford the oven alone, and neither can run the shop alone. Before a single sack of flour is ordered, one question stands between them and their business: who owns what, and who answers for what?

Partnership. Two or more people running a business together and sharing its profits.

Firm. The partners taken together.

The law gives the name partnership to such an arrangement. A partnership is a business owned by two or more people who have agreed to run it together and to share its profits. The people are called partners, and together they are called the firm.2

The law says a partnership begins with agreement.

In Bangladesh the matter is governed by the Partnership Act of 1932. It holds that a partnership arises from an agreement between people, and not from family status or from the mere fact that they own something together.1

What the agreement contains

Partnership deed. The written agreement setting out each partner's rights and duties.

The agreement may be spoken or written, but a written one, called the partnership deed, is wiser. It records what each partner contributes, how profits are divided, and what happens when a partner wishes to leave. Without such a record, every disagreement must be settled from memory, and memory is a poor witness.

An illustrative case, not a real firm.

Case file, illustrative. The first founder contributes the oven and most of the capital. The second contributes the recipes and the customers. They agree to divide profits equally, because each believes the other's part is worth the same. Whether this is fair, and what follows if the shop makes a loss, is what the rest of the chapter examines.

Sharing the profits and the debts

Unless agreed otherwise, profits and losses are shared equally.

Unless the agreement says otherwise, the law treats partners as sharing profits and losses equally, whatever each has put in.

Unlimited liability. A partner's duty to pay the firm's debts from personal property if needed.

There is a second duty, and beginners often overlook it. Each partner is responsible, without limit, for the debts of the firm. If the firm cannot pay, its creditors may look to the personal property of any partner, and not only to what that partner invested.
